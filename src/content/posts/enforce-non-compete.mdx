Non-compete agreements are one of the most contested areas of employment law. Whether you're an employer trying to protect your business or an employee who just received a job offer contingent on signing one, it's essential to understand what these agreements actually mean in practice.

## What Is a Non-Compete Agreement?

A non-compete agreement (also called a restrictive covenant) is a contractual clause that prevents an employee or contractor from working for a competitor or starting a competing business for a defined period after leaving a role.

## The Core Question: Are They Enforceable?

This depends heavily on jurisdiction. Courts in the United States, for example, vary dramatically by state:

- **California** — Non-competes are almost entirely unenforceable. The state's policy favors employee mobility.
- **New York** — Enforced narrowly. Courts use a "reasonableness" test.
- **Florida** — More employer-friendly; reasonableness is presumed if properly drafted.

## What Courts Look For

When evaluating enforceability, courts typically assess:

1. **Legitimate business interest** — Does the employer have a real protectable interest (trade secrets, customer relationships)?
2. **Reasonableness of scope** — Is the geographic restriction, duration, and activity scope proportional?
3. **Adequate consideration** — Was the employee compensated for signing beyond just continued employment?

## Practical Advice

If you're presented with a non-compete, have it reviewed by an employment attorney before signing. The language matters enormously, and a poorly drafted clause can have consequences you don't anticipate.

If you're an employer, work with counsel to narrow your non-competes to the minimum necessary to protect your legitimate interests — overreach invites costly litigation.
